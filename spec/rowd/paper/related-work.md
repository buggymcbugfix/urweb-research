## Related Work

This work is a novel combination of different elements from the literature. Each ingredient exists somewhere; the combination does not, and two of the ingredients are, as far as I know, new in the form we use them. Let me go through the neighbours by what they are about, say what they can say that we cannot and the reverse, and then state what is new.

**Row calculi.** Ur's rows are Harper and Pierce's symmetric concatenation (POPL 1991): `r1 ++ r2` for two *unknown* rows, legal only under a compatibility hypothesis, which is what Ur's `~` guards are. The other tradition is Rémy's (1989–93): a row is built by *asymmetric extension* `{l : τ ; ρ}`, and the row variable `ρ` ranges, by its kind, over rows that lack `l`, so no constraint is ever written; Gaster and Jones (1996) make the same "ρ lacks l" an inferred predicate in a qualified type; Leijen's scoped labels (2005) allow duplicates and need neither. These systems infer completely and cannot concatenate two unknown rows at all. Type-level *restriction* of one label exists at least since Cardelli and Mitchell's "Operations on Records" (1991), `R \ x`; set-valued operators on rows appear as closed computations in Haskell's `row-types` library (`.\\`, `.+`), in PureScript's `Prim.Row.Union`/`Nub` classes with functional dependencies, and in TypeScript's `Omit`/`Exclude`: they compute when both operands are known and are stuck otherwise, exactly the "opaque" treatment of §4.1. Rose (Morris and McKinna, POPL 2019) is the principled version of that family: concatenation is a ternary *predicate* `r1 ⊙ r2 ~ r3`, difference is the predicate read backwards, inference is constraint entailment plus Hindley–Milner generalization. What none of them has is a difference operator `r -- s` with `s` an arbitrary row and a *complete* unifier for the resulting equations. What they have that we do not: Rémy and Rose have principal types with no annotations at all; Leijen and Koka have duplicates, which matter for handler semantics; PureScript's rows are ordered multisets.

**Boolean unification in type inference.** Flix is the only type system I know that solves unification problems modulo Boolean algebra: effects as formulas over pure/impure (Madsen and van de Pol, OOPSLA 2020), nullability (OOPSLA 2021), set formulas over named effects with complement (Lutze et al., ICFP 2023), the engineering (OOPSLA 2023), subeffecting over Boolean algebras (Lee et al., OOPSLA 2025). MLstruct (Parreaux and Chau, OOPSLA 2022) has principal inference in a Boolean algebra of *types*, a different object. Flix can say what we cannot: complement (`~Throw`, "everything but"), hence an exclusion as a standalone set rather than relative to a variable; handlers that remove effects. We can say what Flix cannot: our sets are also record field sets, so `r -- [Id]` at `{Type}` is a type of records, and the same theory types `$r`; and because there is no complement there is no universe, so a library function's index means the same in every program, where Flix had to make its universe symbolic to keep separate compilation (ICFP 2023 §3.6). The algebra itself, Boolean rings *without unit*, is not treated in Martin and Nipkow and not used by Flix; the unit-less variant with the "extra atom" (§5.1–5.2) is a small but real technical contribution.

**Effect systems with rows.** Koka (Leijen, 2014), Links (Hillerström and Lindley, 2016), Frank, Effekt: effects as rows, inferred by first-order row unification, which is complete because the algebra is a free commutative monoid (Koka: multisets). They cannot form an intersection or a difference of two unknown effect rows, so "runs where both can" and "all but E" are not types there, and two open rows cannot be unioned (the effect-poisoning problem OOPSLA 2023 §2.6 attributes to row systems). They have handlers, which we do not attempt. Scala's capture checking (Boruch-Gruszecki, Odersky et al., "Capturing types", TOPLAS 2023) tracks *sets* of capabilities with subset ordering as subtyping and solves subtyping constraints; it expresses upper bounds natively and has no notion of most general unifier; it is about escape and aliasing, not placement.

**Graded monads.** Katsumata (POPL 2014) is the theory; Orchard and Petricek (Haskell 2014) the embedding with type-level sets, where union is a type family and anything with a variable is stuck. Our `eff` is a Katsumata graded monad over a product of two semilattices with the grade algebra solved by the unifier; I know of no graded-monad system whose grades are inferred by Boolean unification.

**Placement.** Links (Cooper, Lindley, Wadler, Yallop, 2006) annotates functions `client`/`server`; Eliom (Radanne, Vouillon, Balat, APLAS 2016) has a typed location system with three locations, `client`, `server` and `base`, where `base` code is usable on both, which is our `where` for two sides; ScalaLoci (Weisenburger, Köhler, Salvaneschi, OOPSLA 2018) has placement types `T on Peer` for *data*; ML5 (Murphy, Crary, Harper, 2007) has modal types `A at w`; Ur/Web itself checks sidedness by analysis. These place *values and functions at one location*; ours indexes *computations by the set of sides they may run on*, intersects along sequencing, infers it, and generalizes to any number of sides. They can say where data lives and type remote references; we only say where code may run, and crossings are explicit primitives.

**Document typing.** XDuce (Hosoya and Pierce) and CDuce (Benzaken, Castagna, Frisch) type XML with regular expression types and set-theoretic subtyping, and can express a content model exactly, with sequence, alternation and repetition. We express only "may stand directly inside one of these" and ancestor flags, which is what the HTML standard's *parents* column says and nothing about order or counts. Ur/Web's label encoding (PLDI 2010 §5, POPL 2015) is the direct ancestor and the thing we fix.

**What is new, stated carefully.** (i) A set-valued difference on rows at every kind, with names-only semantics, in a Harper–Pierce-style row system. (ii) Complete unification at the unit-row kind by Boolean unification over finite sets without unit, with the name-by-name decomposition; the Flix idea moved from effect formulas into a row type system, and shown to be what makes union and intersection of unknown rows inferable where every row system before computes them only when closed. (iii) The observation that `r -- [l]` *is* Rémy's "ρ lacks l" as a term rather than a kind, which is why the guards disappear (next section). (iv) Set-valued placement as the grade of a graded monad, inferred. (v) The same mechanism typing HTML content models from the standard's own table. A reviewer will call (i) and (ii) the contribution and (iii)–(v) applications; the HTML and placement applications are where a reader of the paper sees the difference.

## A note about disjointness.

It does arise mechanically, wherever both rows are known. `[A = int] ++ [B = string]` needs no guard; the prover sees two distinct literal names (PLDI 2010 §4.1). A call `f [[Name = string]] x` discharges `f`'s guard `[[Id] ~ r]` mechanically too, because `r` is instantiated to a literal row. What has to be *written* is a guard on a *variable*:

```
fun f [r ::: {Type}] [[Id] ~ r] (x : $([Id = int] ++ r)) = ...
```

Here `[Id = int] ++ r` is only meaningful if `r` lacks `Id`, and nothing about an arbitrary `r` says so. The guard is a hypothesis about the caller's row, part of `f`'s interface, and it is exactly Harper and Pierce's compatibility constraint: the price of being allowed to write `r1 ++ r2` for two unknown rows at all. The two other traditions avoid writing it in two ways. Rémy puts "lacks `Id`" into the *kind* of the row variable, so `{Id : int ; ρ}` is well formed by construction, at the cost of never concatenating two unknown rows. Gaster–Jones and Rose let the constraint arise from the body and *generalize* it into the inferred type, `f :: (ρ \ Id) => {Id :: Int | ρ} -> ...`; that needs Hindley–Milner generalization of qualified types, which Ur does not do, by the design decision that polymorphism is never inferred (§1.3). So in Ur the constraint is mechanical at every *use* and written at every *definition* that abstracts over a row, which is every helper in Basis and in Warenwirtschaft, hence "pervasive". Could the compiler infer the guards of a definition from its body? Yes, that is Gaster–Jones; Ur chose not to, for the same reason it asks for `:::` binders: the signature is the interface, and the author states it.

Now the connection to difference. `r -- [Id]` is a row that lacks `Id`, as a *term*: it is Rémy's "ρ lacks `Id`" without a kind system to carry it. So

```
fun f [r ::: {Type}] (x : $([Id = int] ++ (r -- [Id]))) = ...
```

needs no guard, because `[Id = int] ~ (r -- [Id])` holds by the rule `(c1 -- c2) ~ c2` with the sides swapped, for every `r`. This is why the HTML guards vanish: `[Div] ++ c` written through `union` is `[Div] ++ (c -- [Div])`. But the two signatures do not mean the same thing. With the guard, a caller whose row already has `Id` is *rejected*; with the difference, its `Id` is silently *dropped* and replaced, the way a record update would. For HTML contexts that is what we want (a fragment that already "has `Div`" is fine under a `div`). For form field names it is not: two textboxes called `X` in one form must be an error, and that is what the `bind` row's disjointness obligations do today. So the honest statement for the notes: difference removes the guards that were only encoding "lacks" (every `ctx` guard in Basis and the `other ~ [Table, Tr]` family), and guards stay where a clash is a bug the type checker should report (`use`/`bind` rows, record concatenation in user code). I will add that distinction to §6.3, since the earlier text says "guards go" without the qualification.
